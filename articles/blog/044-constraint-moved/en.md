# Scoring the Fertilizer-Crisis Forecast

## Conclusion

On 25 April this site wrote that [phosphate fertilizer would become unobtainable in Japan](https://aiseed.dev/en/blog/phosphate-crisis-2027/): **inventories exhausted in the autumn 2026 planting season, phosphate fertilizer effectively unobtainable in 2027**, supply down to 11–16% of normal.

The pathway was the **sulfur trap**. Close Hormuz and sulfur stops. Without sulfur there is no sulfuric acid. Without sulfuric acid the rock does not dissolve, and phosphate fertilizer cannot be made.

Three months have passed. Here is the scorecard against the facts.

**The pathway was right. The conclusion was wrong.** Sulfur really did stop, and phosphate production really did halt — elsewhere in the world. But the shortage that showed up in Japan was not phosphate. It was **nitrogen**.

**The constraint has not disappeared. It moved.**

## What Was Right — Sulfur Really Did Stop

Start with the side that held. The sulfur trap was not a paper exercise.

**Prices.** As of 28 May 2026, Middle East sulfur was **$815–820 a tonne** FOB and Brazil landed at **$1,200** CFR. The historical low-price regime was $50–150, so this is **five to eight times normal**. Liquid sulfur has nearly tripled since the end of 2024.

**Three causes stacked.** The effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Russia's sulfur export ban — imposed in November 2025 and extended to June 2026, triggered by strikes on the Astrakhan facility, which alone accounts for about 60% of Russian sulfur. And China throttling sulfuric acid exports — 4.6 million tonnes in 2025, projected at roughly 1.2 million in 2026.

**And production actually stopped.** Egypt's EFIC halted granular SSP output. Brazil's Mosaic paused part of its phosphate rock and chemical operations. In both cases the reason was sulfur procurement.

The cost pass-through also behaved as estimated: moving sulfur from $100 to $500 a tonne adds roughly **$160 per tonne** to DAP production costs.

The pathway from sulfur to phosphate was real.

## What Was Wrong — The Shortage Landed on Nitrogen

But the numbers that showed up in Japan's ledger pointed elsewhere.

Here is [Zen-Noh's autumn fertilizer pricing announced on 15 May](https://www.zennoh.or.jp/press/release/2026/109288.html), covering June through October.

| Product | Change |
|---|---|
| **Imported urea (large grain)** | **+14.5%** |
| Potassium sulfate | +7.8% |
| Potassium chloride | +7.3% |
| High-grade compound (15-15-15) | +5.0% |
| **Superphosphate** | **+4.9%** |
| **Double superphosphate** | **+4.5%** |

**Phosphate was supposed to be the tightest. The actual increase was one third of nitrogen's. The order of scarcity was inverted.**

International prices point the same way. In April 2026 urea rose **22.7%** month on month and ammonia **18.2%**. DAP, by contrast, went $619 in January, $627 in February, $658 in March — **up 5%**. The World Bank's view is that DAP rises about 6% in 2026 and then **falls about 10% in 2027** as new capacity comes online. That is the opposite direction from "unobtainable in 2027."

Why did nitrogen rise? **Qatar suspended production of urea, ammonia and sulfur together** after damage to its facilities. And **Iran halted ammonia production.**

## Why Phosphate Held

Four reasons.

**One. China's halt had an expiry date.** On 11 December 2025, under National Development and Reform Commission guidance, exports were "temporarily suspended until August 2026"; from 14 March customs stopped accepting export declarations. This site read that as a structural rupture. **It was a measure with a date on it.**

**Two. The stockpile worked.** Against a target of three months of annual demand for phosphate ammonium and potassium chloride by FY2027, potassium chloride had reached target and phosphate ammonium stood at 2.4 months — a buffer thick enough to absorb precisely a period like this one.

**Three. Diversification worked.** Phosphate ammonium imports are 72% China, but Morocco supplies 21% and Israel 7%. Not all of it was China.

**Four. The seasonality was already priced in.** China's export restrictions cluster from January to April, and Zen-Noh procures on the assumption that nothing arrives from China in that window. **It was not an unexpected halt. It was an expected one.**

## What Was Misread

This is the heart of the piece.

**The same Hormuz shock severed both sulfur and gas.** This site traced sulfur → sulfuric acid → phosphate carefully, and took gas → ammonia → nitrogen lightly. Sulfur passes through Hormuz. So do LNG, natural gas, ammonia and urea. Only one of the pipes was being watched.

And there is an irony. **Nitrogen, the one that had successfully diversified, was the one that got hit.**

Urea's dependence on China fell from 27% in FY2020 to 3% in FY2024. Malaysia now supplies 74%, Vietnam 10% — a diversification success story, in contrast to phosphate's 72% concentration on China. And urea still went up 14.5%, with the domestic price climbing from ¥64,500 a tonne in January 2024 to ¥93,100 in March 2026.

The reason is simple. **The counterparty changed; the resource did not.** Urea is made from natural gas. Whether you buy from Malaysia or from Qatar, the price is set by global gas and ammonia supply and demand, and the centre of that is the Middle East.

**Diversification meant changing the resource, not the counterparty.** Rearranging the list of sellers changes nothing if every one of them draws from the same well. When the well runs dry, they all run dry together.

## What Has Not Changed

Having written the miss, write the unchanged parts precisely too.

**The shape of the dependence has not moved at all.** 72% of phosphate ammonium still comes from China. Urea's feedstock is still Middle Eastern gas. This year held because of stockpiles, diversification, and an expiry date on China's measure — **not because the dependence was resolved.**

Nor did this research overturn the long-run structure: the triple cost of declining ore grade (cadmium removal, excess sulfuric acid consumption, radioactive waste); peak phosphorus, which several models place around 2033; the fact that only 1–2% of applied phosphate is absorbed while the rest binds into the soil; and the biology by which mycorrhizal fungi can mobilise that bound phosphorus.

**The case for shifting to natural farming does not rest on a collapse date. It rests on the structure.** Missing the date does not mean missing the structure — but trying to move people with a date was the mistake.

## What the Spring 2027 Season Looks Like

Spring fertilizer pricing, covering November through May, is normally set around November. It has not been announced, but the inputs are already on the table.

**Phosphate is heading looser — in world prices.** The World Bank sees DAP rising about 6% in 2026 and then **falling about 10% in 2027** as new capacity comes online and exports recover.

**Where Japan actually buys is a separate question.** Phosphate ammonium comes from China (72%), Morocco (21%) and Israel (7%) — those three and no others. All three are in trouble right now.

China is under an export halt until August, and whether it resumes is the largest fork. Israel is 7%, and at war. And Morocco turned out to be the most important finding.

**On 3 April, OCP announced maintenance work reported to affect up to 30% of its production capacity in the second quarter. The reason was tight procurement of sulfur and ammonia.** Morocco imports **52% of its sulfur from the Middle East**. **The supplier Japan turned to in place of China sits inside the same sulfur trap.**

Saudi Arabia's Ma'aden holds 6 million tonnes a year of capacity and shipped around 500,000 tonnes a month. But it does not appear in Japan's import record — and its loading port at Ras Al-Khair sits inside the Persian Gulf, so **its cargoes transit the Strait of Hormuz.** It already holds a five-year contract with India for 3.1 million tonnes a year.

**So spring phosphate rests, in practice, on China's decision in August.** The 2.4 months of phosphate ammonium in the stockpile is the cushion for the wait.

**Nitrogen stays elevated.** The World Bank expects urea to rise nearly **60% on average in 2026**, then ease in 2027 as Middle East exports resume and gas costs cool. But the easing carries a weight: **damage to Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG complex will take years to repair**, and the view is that urea prices stay elevated through 2027 even once shipping resumes. The Gulf accounted for **36%** of global urea exports in 2023–25, with Iran and Qatar the largest exporters.

New capacity is accumulating: roughly **4.6 million tonnes a year** of additional ammonia over the next two years, and Qatar plans to lift urea capacity from 6 to over **12.4 million tonnes a year**. But these land in 2027 and beyond.

**On the ordering, then: nitrogen above and phosphate below is likely to persist into spring — but the phosphate side is the more fragile of the two, because it rests on a single decision in Beijing rather than on a market.** Zen-Noh itself has pointed to the possibility of a further increase in spring fertilizer if Middle East tensions persist, and calls the raw-material outlook "extremely difficult" to forecast.

Two variables sit on the Japanese side: **the exchange rate**, and **whether China actually resumes in August.**

## Do Not Read Price as Supply

Here is the hole this piece itself nearly fell into.

In draft, I had written that phosphate was loosening. The grounds were three: the World Bank's 10% decline for 2027, DAP's international price up only 5%, and Zen-Noh's autumn phosphate at +4.9%. **Every one of those is a price series. None of them measures whether the material reaches Japan.**

The error had three layers.

**One. World price was confused with Japanese availability.** The world DAP price is set by the large flows — Morocco to India and Brazil, Saudi Arabia to India. Japan's procurement is set by three suppliers and no others. **The world market can loosen while those three jam simultaneously, and nothing arrives.** The World Bank is looking at a market; Japan is facing a basket.

**Two. Price faces backwards.** The June-to-October prices Zen-Noh set in May are the prices of product made from material secured earlier still. Superphosphate is manufactured domestically, which lags further. **"The price is calm" does not mean "supply is secured." It means "procurement through yesterday was sufficient."**

**Three, and most important. The adjustment is happening in quantity, not in price.**

Do the arithmetic. The figure cited earlier: moving sulfur from $100 to $500 a tonne adds about $160 a tonne to DAP costs. But actual sulfur has gone from a normal $50–150 to **$815–820**. Extend at the same ratio and the cost increase runs into the **hundreds of dollars** a tonne.

Meanwhile DAP moved from $619 in January to $658 in March — **thirty-nine dollars.**

**Where did the difference go? It was not passed through. Production stopped instead.** That is exactly what Egypt's EFIC halting granular SSP and Brazil's Mosaic pausing part of its operations are. When cost exceeds what the product will fetch, the producer who cannot make it does not raise the price. **They stop making it.**

**So the adjustment is running through quantity, not price.** And an adjustment in quantity reaches the price late — it arrives all at once, on the day the inventory runs out.

**A quiet price is not evidence that supply and demand are loose. It can equally be evidence that the adjustment is happening somewhere other than the price.** I read it as evidence of easing.

And then it becomes visible. **This is the mirror image of the error in the April piece.** That piece read a global mechanism — the sulfur trap — and concluded a Japan-specific catastrophe. The draft of this piece read a global price forecast and concluded a Japan-specific easing. **Both mix the world layer with the Japan layer.** The scorer was making the same kind of mistake as the scored.

**Read the layers separately.** That, in the end, is the one method this scorecard yields.

With that said, the implication for a farm is about allocation, not price. Nitrogen stays high; phosphate swings on a decision in Beijing. **Either way, whoever has reduced their dependence on purchased fertilizer comes out ahead.** Legume rotation, green manure, compost, soil microbes — their relative advantage rises whether prices climb or supply thins. That is not an ideological argument. It falls out of both the price list and the procurement list.

## If the Switch to Nitrophosphate Happens

If sulfuric acid is unobtainable, switch to a process that does not use it. The thought is natural, and the process exists: the **nitrophosphate process**, also called the **Odda process**, devised by Erling Johnson in Odda, Norway, in 1927.

The principle is simple. Digest phosphate rock with dilute **nitric acid** instead of sulfuric. What forms is a mixture of phosphoric acid and calcium nitrate; cool it below 0°C and the calcium nitrate crystallises out and can be separated. Neutralise the remaining filtrate with ammonia and you have a compound fertilizer; add potash and you have NPK. **No sulfuric acid, and no phosphogypsum.** It looks like a perfect escape.

But five things follow from the switch.

**One. It eats nitrogen at two points.** The nitric acid that digests the rock is made from ammonia. And the filtrate is neutralised with ammonia. The calcium nitrate emerges as a nitrogen fertilizer, so it is not simple consumption — but **the direction is unambiguously toward more ammonia demand.** You would be pulling harder on the very thing that is currently tightest.

**Two — and this is the essential point. Nitrogen and phosphate become tied to the same resource.** Under the sulfuric route, phosphate is tied to sulfur, a by-product of oil refining. Under the nitric route, phosphate is tied to ammonia, which is made from natural gas — **the identical resource as nitrogen.**

Two pipes become one. If gas stops, nitrogen and phosphate stop **together**. Part of the reason phosphate held this year is precisely that it hung on a different pipe. **Switching to nitrophosphate means voluntarily discarding the last diversification remaining between the nutrients.**

**Three. The shape of the traded good changes.** What comes out is NPK compound, not DAP or MAP. Japan imports phosphate ammonium and blends high-analysis compound domestically; if the world's traded good becomes NPK, **both the freedom to set one's own ratios and the domestic blending step thin out** (this part is inference).

**Four. The winners are Europe and Russia.** The technology and the plants sit with Yara in Norway (5.2 million tonnes of European NPK capacity, over 80% of its global total), EuroChem Antwerpen in Belgium (nitric acid, NPK and nitrophosphoric acid units), and PhosAgro and EuroChem in Russia. **Russia is the one country holding all three: gas, phosphate rock, and the process.** Whether replacing dependence on China with dependence on Russia counts as an improvement is an entirely separate judgment.

**Five. And the place you land is currently being bombed.**

Nitric acid does not only make fertilizer. **It makes explosives.** Nitric acid and ammonia are feedstocks for propellant and shells. Which makes plants that produce nitric acid military targets in wartime.

Nevinnomyssk Azot in Stavropol Krai, Russia's largest producer of nitrogen fertilizer and ammonia, makes ammonia, nitrogen fertilizers and **nitric acid**. It has been reported struck by drones repeatedly: August 2025, December 2025, January 2026, March 2026, and again on 15–16 May and 20 May 2026. Novomoskovsk Azot in Tula Oblast was struck on 14 June and 26 June, and again on **25–26 July** — two days before this piece was written.

The reason is also reported. Between 2022 and 2024 the two plants supplied nitric and acetic acid to a facility manufacturing explosives for artillery shells. **Fertilizer and explosives share an entrance.**

So switching to nitrophosphate also means **moving phosphate supply onto the kind of plant that becomes a military target.** Escape sulfur, land on gas, merge with nitrogen — and find that the junction is under fire precisely because it also feeds munitions.

This is also part of why nitrogen is tight this year. **Qatar and Iran are not the only producers that stopped. One of the world's major sources of nitrogen fertilizer is being struck intermittently.**

And there is the matter of time. **A sulfuric acid plant cannot be converted into a nitric acid plant.** It is different equipment. The capital cycle runs in years, which makes this a question for the 2030s, not for 2027.

So nitrophosphate is **an escape route, but not an exit.** Escape sulfur and you land on gas; where you land you merge into the same pipe as nitrogen; and that pipe is currently being attacked because it is also a feedstock for explosives. This is where the thesis of this piece — the constraint does not vanish, it moves — appears in its most concentrated form.

## What to Watch

**August.** Whether China resumes phosphate fertilizer exports on schedule. This is the largest fork. If it resumes, this scorecard becomes firmer.

**November.** Zen-Noh's spring pricing, and whether the ordering of nitrogen and phosphate holds.

**The autumn season (September–November).** Actual supply and demand — whether shortages or allocation appear.

**The escape route in process.** Whether actual movement toward the nitrophosphate route appears. As the previous section sets out, that is a trade of sulfur for gas, and the capital cycle makes it a question for the 2030s. There is precedent — the same discussion arose during the sulfur crisis of the 1970s and 80s.

And the condition under which this piece is wrong. **If China does not resume in August and shortages appear in autumn, this scorecard is overturned.** If it is, that will be written here.
